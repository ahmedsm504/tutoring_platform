Every day, many families pay for Quran lessons: a monthly fee at a local maktab, a salary for a teacher at an Islamic school, or a subscription for online one-to-one classes. Some parents hesitate: Is it right to pay for the Quran? Shouldn't teaching the Book of Allah be free, for the sake of Allah? And some teachers feel uneasy about taking money for something they love to do as worship.

This is an old question. Scholars discussed it from the first century of Islam, the schools of law differed on it, and then the practice of Muslims in most lands settled on permitting a wage for teaching. This article presents the views and their evidence, distinguishes between situations that people often mix up, and gives important etiquette for teachers and parents.

## Three Different Situations
Before the views, we need to separate three situations:
1. **Taking a wage for teaching the Quran**: a teacher teaches reading, tajweed and memorisation in exchange for payment. This is the main subject of this article.
2. **Taking a wage for reciting the Quran itself**: for example, hiring a reciter to complete a recitation for a deceased person or at an event. This is a different situation with its own ruling.
3. **Taking a wage for ruqyah with the Quran**: there is a specific authentic text about this.

These are also different from **a stipend from the public treasury or an endowment (waqf)**, given to a teacher by the state or a charitable trust. Almost all scholars permit this, because it is not a wage under a contract but support for someone who devotes themselves to a service for Muslims.

## View One: Permitted (the Majority)
The **Maliki and Shafi'i** schools, the Hanbalis in one narration on which many of them gave fatwa, and the **later Hanafi scholars** hold that **taking a wage for teaching the Quran is permitted**. The later Hanafis adopted this view when they saw that stipends from the public treasury had stopped, and feared that teaching the Quran would be lost if teachers could not earn a living.

### Their evidence
**First**: al-Bukhari reported from Ibn 'Abbas that the Prophet ﷺ said: **"The most deserving thing for which you take a wage is the Book of Allah."** This was said in the context of a Companion who performed ruqyah with Al-Fatihah on a man stung by a scorpion in exchange for some sheep. Some Companions disliked it, and the Prophet ﷺ approved it.

**Second**: in al-Bukhari and Muslim, in the hadith of Abu Sa'id al-Khudri about the same ruqyah, the Prophet ﷺ said: "Divide it, and give me a share with you." He took a share to reassure them and to show that it was lawful.

**Third**: in al-Bukhari and Muslim, in the hadith of Sahl ibn Sa'd about the woman who offered herself in marriage to the Prophet ﷺ, he married her to a man who had nothing to give as a dowry, and said: **"I have married her to you for what you have of the Quran"**, meaning that he would teach her what he knew of the Quran. Teaching the Quran was made a dowry, and a dowry is a form of compensation, which shows that teaching the Quran can be compensated.

**Fourth**: teaching requires effort and time; the teacher dedicates themselves to it and deserves compensation for their time and effort, just as a teacher of writing or arithmetic does.

**Fifth**: public benefit. If teachers were forbidden from taking a wage, most would turn to other work to earn a living, and teaching the Quran would be neglected in many places.

## View Two: Not Permitted (Abu Hanifah and One Narration from Ahmad)
**Imam Abu Hanifah** and the early Hanafi scholars, and the Hanbalis in another narration, held that **taking a wage for teaching the Quran is not permitted**, considering it among the acts of devotion for which one may not be hired, like the call to prayer and leading prayer in the original position of their school.

### Their evidence
**First**: the hadith of 'Ubadah ibn as-Samit, who taught a man from the people of the Suffah the Quran, and the man gave him a bow as a gift. 'Ubadah asked the Prophet ﷺ, who said: "If you would like to be collared with a collar of fire, then accept it." Reported by Abu Dawud and Ibn Majah. Hadith scholars differ over its authenticity: a number of experts weakened it because of a narrator in its chain, while others considered it acceptable through its supporting routes.

**Second**: the hadith of 'Abdur-Rahman ibn Shibl that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Recite the Quran, and do not go to extremes in it, do not neglect it, do not eat by it, and do not seek to increase your wealth by it." Reported by Ahmad and graded authentic by al-Albani.

**Third**: teaching the Quran is worship, and one is not hired for worship; taking a wage is seen as conflicting with sincerity.

## Reconciling the Evidence
The majority responded to these proofs:
- **The hadith of the bow**: if authentic, it is understood to mean that 'Ubadah had volunteered to teach for the sake of Allah from the start, so when a gift came afterwards he was told not to accept it so as not to spoil his intention; or that the people of the Suffah were poor and nothing should be taken from them. This differs from someone who agrees a known fee for teaching from the outset.
- **"Do not eat by it"**: understood as begging with the Quran and using it to amass worldly gain, such as reciting in the streets to be given money, not a known fee for teaching in return for effort and time.
- **Conflict with sincerity**: a wage does not necessarily spoil the intention. A teacher may take what suffices them while their intention is spreading the Quran, just as a fighter receives spoils while his intention is to make the word of Allah supreme.

## What Has Become the Established Practice?
In most Muslim lands, the practice has settled on **permitting a wage for teaching the Quran**. The later Hanafis gave fatwa for it out of necessity, and contemporary fiqh councils and fatwa bodies follow it. Teachers in schools, memorisation centres and institutes receive salaries, and private teachers charge fees, without objection from the major scholars of our time.

## A Different Case: Paying Someone to Recite for the Dead
**Hiring a reciter to recite the Quran** and gift its reward to a deceased person, or to recite at a funeral gathering or event, is a more disputed case:
- **Many scholars forbade it**, including Ibn Taymiyyah. They argued that if the reciter recites for money, there is no reward in the first place, so what is gifted to the deceased? Recitation itself is a pure act of worship for which one is not hired, unlike teaching, which brings a lasting benefit to the student.
- **Some later scholars of certain schools permitted it** with conditions.

The difference between the two cases is clear: **in teaching, the student gains knowledge that stays with them**, so the wage is in return for a tangible benefit. **In mere recitation, the payer gains nothing except reward**, and reward is not obtained when the intention is money.

## Ruqyah for a Fee
Ruqyah with the Quran for a fee is permitted by the authentic hadith of the man stung by a scorpion, as mentioned above. But scholars have warned about some limits: it must not turn into a trade that exploits the needs of the sick, fees must not be excessive, and claims should not be made that are not established.

## Online Quran Classes
Teaching the Quran online takes the same ruling as teaching in person: it is **teaching in return for a wage**, and the fee is for the teacher's time and effort in correction and follow-up. This includes:
- Paid one-to-one sessions.
- Monthly subscriptions on platforms.
- Recorded tajweed courses.
- Paid ijazah programmes, provided that the fee is for teaching and supervision, not for "buying" the ijazah itself. An ijazah is a certification that cannot be bought and should only be granted to someone who has mastered the recitation.

## Etiquette for Teachers
Permissibility does not mean a teacher should treat the Quran like any other product. Scholars have mentioned important etiquette:
1. **Purify your intention**: your first aim should be spreading the Quran and seeking reward from Allah; money is a means of sufficiency and freeing your time. Imam an-Nawawi wrote in *at-Tibyan* that a teacher should intend Allah's pleasure by teaching and not seek a worldly purpose through it.
2. **Fair fees**: do not overcharge, exploiting people's desire to teach their children the Quran.
3. **Equal quality**: do not give more attention in correction to the student who pays more; the Quran is one and the trust is one.
4. **Discounts or free places for the poor**: one of the greatest things a teacher can offer is free or reduced places for those who cannot pay.
5. **Honesty with time**: a paid session is the student's right, so do not cut it short or fill it with other things.
6. **Honest assessment**: do not flatter a student about their level to keep them paying, and never grant a certificate or ijazah to someone who has not earned it.
7. **Do not attach your heart to money**: if students decrease or income stops, do not abandon teaching; keep spreading the Quran as much as you can.

## Advice for Parents
1. **Do not hesitate to pay a Quran teacher**: it is one of the best ways to spend your money, giving your child knowledge that stays with them for life and that you can hope will be ongoing charity for you.
2. **Look for quality, not the cheapest option**: careful teaching saves years of correcting mistakes later.
3. **Respect the teacher and pay on time**: delaying a teacher's wage is a form of injustice. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries," reported by Ibn Majah and graded authentic by al-Albani through its combined routes.
4. **If you can, support others**: sponsor the Quran education of an orphan or a poor child, which is one of the greatest forms of charity.

## How Did Quran Teachers Live Throughout History?
Teaching the Quran in Islamic history was not always purely voluntary. Teachers were supported in various ways:
- **In the time of the Prophet ﷺ and the Companions**: many Companions taught the Quran voluntarily, and some received what sufficed them from the public treasury. 'Umar ibn al-Khattab sent teachers to the new cities and provided them with stipends.
- **The kuttab**: Quran schools spread across villages and cities, and the teacher received payment from parents, in kind or in money, or a gift when a child completed a portion of the Quran or the whole.
- **Endowments**: Muslims established vast endowments for teaching the Quran, and teachers received their salaries from their income. This was one of the greatest means by which the Quran was preserved in hearts across the centuries.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Is it halal to take money for teaching the Quran?
Yes, according to the majority of scholars and the established practice, based on the hadith "The most deserving thing for which you take a wage is the Book of Allah."

### Is the hadith of the bow authentic?
Hadith scholars differ over it; even if authentic, the majority understand it to refer to someone who volunteered for Allah's sake, not to an agreed fee.

### Is paying for online Quran classes permissible?
Yes. It takes the same ruling as in-person teaching, as the fee is for the teacher's time and effort.

### Can I pay someone to recite the Quran for my deceased parent?
Many scholars forbade this, because recitation for money carries no reward to gift; it is better to recite yourself or give charity on their behalf.

### Can a ruqyah practitioner charge a fee?
Yes, according to the authentic hadith, but without exploitation, excessive fees or false claims.

### Can an ijazah be bought?
No. A fee may be paid for teaching and supervision, but the ijazah is a certification of mastery that is granted only to those who have earned it.

## Conclusion
The majority of scholars, and the established practice of Muslims, permit taking a wage for teaching the Quran, based on the hadith "The most deserving thing for which you take a wage is the Book of Allah" and the dowry of teaching the Quran, while Abu Hanifah and the early Hanafis forbade it before their later scholars permitted it out of need. Paid recitation for the dead is a different and more disputed case. Teachers should keep their intention pure, their fees fair and their standards honest, and parents should pay willingly and on time, knowing that few things they spend on will last as long as the Quran in their child's heart.
