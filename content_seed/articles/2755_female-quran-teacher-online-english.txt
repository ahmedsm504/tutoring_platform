"I want a female teacher for my daughter." "I am a mother with small children and cannot attend a class at the mosque. Is there a sister who teaches online?" "I want to correct my recitation, but I feel shy reciting in front of a male teacher." These are some of the most common messages we receive.

Online learning has opened a wide door for women and girls. Circumstances that once kept them away from Quran classes, such as young children, distance, work, or living in a country with no nearby Quran school, no longer need to stand in the way. This guide helps you choose the right female teacher for yourself or your daughter, and answers the questions sisters ask us most often.

## Why Many Families Prefer a Female Teacher

### Comfort and confidence
Many girls, especially teenagers, feel more relaxed with a female teacher. They ask questions freely, make mistakes without embarrassment, and talk openly about what they find difficult. Feeling comfortable is the foundation of learning.

### Freedom at home
With a female teacher, a student can attend her lesson at home in her everyday clothes, and turn the camera on when the teacher needs to see the lips and mouth to correct the articulation of certain letters.

### A role model
A female teacher who has memorised and mastered the Quran is a living example for a girl: a woman who learned, mastered and now teaches the Book of Allah. Many girls come to love the Quran because they love their teacher.

### Understanding a woman's circumstances
A female teacher understands the realities of a student who is also a mother: the baby who suddenly cries, the household responsibilities, the days of menstruation. She can be flexible in ways that make the difference between continuing and giving up.

## How to Choose a Qualified Female Teacher

### 1. Accuracy comes first
The teacher must recite **accurately with Tajweed**, and ideally hold an **ijazah** in the Quran with a connected chain of transmission. Ask directly. Accuracy shows quickly in a trial lesson: does she recite correctly, and does she notice and correct your subtle mistakes?

### 2. Experience with your age group
Teaching a six-year-old girl is very different from teaching a fifteen-year-old or a forty-year-old mother. Ask about her experience with your age group, and how she deals with a restless child or with an adult who feels shy about mistakes.

### 3. Teaching style and patience
A good teacher:
- corrects gently, without sarcasm or sighing;
- explains again in different ways when something is not understood;
- encourages every bit of progress, however small;
- respects adult students and does not treat them like children.

### 4. Reliability
She arrives on time, plans her lessons (review, new material, homework), and tracks progress.

### 5. Flexibility
She can adjust the time when something urgent comes up, takes your circumstances into account, and adapts the plan when it is not working.

### 6. Experience with non-Arabic speakers
If you or your daughter grew up speaking English or another language, a teacher who knows how to teach Arabic sounds to non-Arabic speakers will save you a lot of frustration. Letters such as ح, ع, خ, غ, ق, ض and ط need patient, step-by-step explanation.

## Questions to Ask in the Trial Lesson

1. Do you have an ijazah? In which narration?
2. What is your experience with children, teenagers or adult women?
3. How do you usually structure a lesson?
4. What do you do when a student forgets quickly?
5. Do you give homework? How much time does it need each day?
6. How will I (or my daughter) see progress?
7. What do we do during the days of menstruation?

## Privacy and Safety in Online Lessons

Privacy is one of the biggest concerns for mothers. These are the safeguards to ask about in any online academy:
- **Are lessons one-to-one?** A one-to-one lesson is only between the teacher and the student.
- **Are lessons recorded?** They should not be recorded without prior permission.
- **How do teacher and student communicate?** Ideally inside the academy's platform under supervision, not through personal phone numbers.
- **Are students' photos or voices ever used?** Never without consent.

At our academy these are part of our quality standards: no recording without permission, no sharing of data, and communication between teacher and student or parent inside the platform under the supervision of our team.

## Practical Solutions for Women's Circumstances

### Mothers with young children
- Choose a time when the children nap or when your husband is home.
- It is fine to have a small child next to you during the lesson; an understanding teacher will not mind.
- A focused 30-minute lesson works better than a long, interrupted one.
- Review your memorisation while doing housework by listening and repeating.

### Working women
- Evening or weekend lessons.
- Two lessons a week are usually enough, with a short daily review.
- Listen to your new passage on the way to work.

### The days of menstruation
Whether a menstruating woman may recite the Quran is a matter of scholarly difference. Some scholars prohibit recitation during this time, while others permit reciting from memory, especially when there is a fear of forgetting or when she is a teacher or student, while the majority hold that she should not touch the Mushaf directly. In practice, many teachers turn the lesson on these days into:
- listening and theoretical Tajweed;
- revision from memory for those who follow the permissive view;
- studying the meanings and tafsir of the verses being memorised;
- adhkar and supplications.

Ask a scholar you trust in your community which view to follow; a good teacher will respect your choice.

### Living outside the Arab world
- Book lessons in your local time and double-check the time difference with the teacher, especially when clocks change for daylight saving.
- If your daughter speaks English more than Arabic, tell the teacher so she starts at the right level.

## What Girls Learn With Their Teacher

Depending on age and level:
- **Ages 5–7**: letters and vowels, short surahs by repetition, simple daily adhkar, with games and activities.
- **Ages 8–12**: correct reading from the Mushaf, basic Tajweed, memorising Juz' Amma and Juz' Tabarak, stories of the Prophets.
- **Ages 13 and above**: a structured memorisation plan, mastering Tajweed, reflecting on the meanings and connecting the Quran to daily life, and possibly preparing for an ijazah.

## What Adult Sisters Learn

- **Correcting recitation**: the most common and most important starting point.
- **Memorisation**: with a plan that fits your time, starting with the surahs you use in prayer and adhkar.
- **Theory and practice of Tajweed**: especially for mothers who want to teach their own children.
- **Ijazah**: for those who have mastered recitation and want a certified chain of transmission.

## Role Models From Our History

Women have always carried the Quran. Aisha, the Mother of the Believers, was among the most knowledgeable of the Companions. The written pages on which the Quran was first compiled in the time of Abu Bakr were kept with Hafsah, the Mother of the Believers, and Uthman later took them from her to produce the copies of the Mushaf, as reported in Sahih al-Bukhari. In every generation after them, there were women who memorised, mastered, taught and gave ijazah in the Quran. When a sister learns the Quran today, she walks a path that great women walked before her.

## Common Mistakes When Choosing a Teacher

1. **Choosing on price alone.** Accuracy and teaching style matter more than a small difference in fees.
2. **Skipping the trial lesson.** One lesson reveals a lot.
3. **Staying with a teacher you are not comfortable with.** If there is no connection after several lessons, ask politely for a change.
4. **Expecting very fast results.** Mastery takes time and patience.
5. **No practice between lessons.** The lesson alone is not enough without daily review.

## Tips for Mothers Supporting Their Daughters

- Sit nearby for the first few lessons, then give your daughter and her teacher some space.
- Ask your daughter about the lesson with genuine interest, and ask her to recite what she learned to you.
- Do not compare her with siblings or cousins.
- Stay in regular contact with the teacher to know what your daughter needs at home.
- Be a role model: if she sees you reading and learning the Quran, she will love it too.

## What a Lesson With a Female Teacher Looks Like

Here is a typical 30-minute lesson with a twelve-year-old girl who is memorising:

1. **Greeting (2 minutes)**: how she is, and whether she did her review.
2. **Recitation check (10 minutes)**: she recites what she memorised last time; the teacher corrects mistakes and notes them.
3. **Tajweed point (5 minutes)**: one rule with examples from the same page.
4. **New memorisation (10 minutes)**: the teacher recites the new passage verse by verse, the student repeats, then links it to what came before.
5. **Homework (3 minutes)**: what to review, what to memorise, and how.

With younger girls each segment is shorter, with games and interactive activities in between. With adult sisters the lesson often leans more toward correcting recitation and discussing meanings.

## A Suggested Plan for a Sister Starting From Scratch

| Stage | Approximate time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Fixing the foundations | 1–2 months | Articulation points, correcting Al-Fatihah and the short surahs you recite in prayer |
| Core Tajweed | 2–3 months | Noon and meem sakinah, the types of madd, qalqalah, applied on Juz' Amma |
| Steady memorisation | Ongoing | Half a page to a page a week or more, with daily review |
| Reflection | With every passage | Reading the meaning of what you memorise from a short tafsir |

These timings vary from one sister to another; what matters is consistency, not speed.

## Is It Working? Signs After One Month

After a month of lessons, ask yourself:
- Has my (or my daughter's) recitation clearly improved?
- Does my daughter look forward to the lesson, or avoid it?
- Have I received clear feedback on level and plan?
- Is the teacher punctual and well prepared?
- Is the homework realistic for the time we have?

If most answers are yes, you have found the right teacher. If not, talk openly with the teacher first, and then with the academy if needed.

## Holding On to Memorisation in a Busy Home

- **Put the passage where you see it**: a printed card on the fridge or by the mirror.
- **Use your prayers**: recite what you memorised in your sunnah prayers; it becomes daily revision without extra time.
- **Before sleep**: read today's passage twice before you sleep.
- **A study partner**: agree with a friend or sister to test each other once a week by phone.
