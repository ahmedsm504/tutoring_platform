"My daughter is clever, memorizes surahs quickly by listening, but when she opens the Mushaf she freezes. She confuses ba' and ta', reads words backwards and loses the line. Her school says she may have dyslexia. Will she ever read the Quran properly?" We hear versions of this from parents often, especially families in the UK, USA and Canada where dyslexia is more commonly identified at school.

The encouraging truth is this: **a child with dyslexia can learn to read the Quran and can memorize it**, often very well. But they usually need a different route from the one that suits most children: a route that separates memorization from reading, teaches letters through several senses at once, adapts the page, and protects the child's confidence along the way.

This guide is educational. If you suspect dyslexia, a formal assessment by an educational psychologist or specialist teacher is the right first step, and everything here can work alongside their recommendations.

## What Is Dyslexia?

Dyslexia is a specific learning difficulty that mainly affects reading and spelling: connecting letters to sounds, decoding words quickly and accurately, and sometimes working memory and processing speed. It is **not** a sign of low intelligence or laziness. Many children with dyslexia are bright, creative and verbally strong.

### Signs you might notice in Quran reading
- Confusing letters that look alike, especially those that differ only in dots (ب ت ث, ج ح خ, د ذ, ر ز, س ش, ص ض, ط ظ, ع غ, ف ق).
- Reversing or reordering letters within a word.
- Guessing a word from its first letter.
- Skipping lines or reading the same line twice.
- Very slow, effortful reading even after a lot of practice.
- A big gap between what they can memorize by ear and what they can read.

## Why the Quran Can Be Especially Challenging

Several features of Quranic Arabic add to the load for a dyslexic reader:
- **Dots matter**: many letters share a shape and differ only in the number or position of dots.
- **Letters change shape** at the start, middle and end of a word.
- **Right-to-left direction**, which may be new for a child who reads English.
- **Dense pages**: a standard Mushaf page has 15 lines of small script with diacritics above and below.
- **Uthmani spelling**: small alifs, special signs and stopping marks add more visual information.

None of these are reasons to give up. They are reasons to plan carefully.

## Principle 1: Separate Memorization From Reading

The single most important decision is to stop treating reading and memorization as one task. For many dyslexic children, memorization by ear is a strength. Let them use it.

### Memorizing through listening and talqeen
The teacher or parent recites, the child repeats, verse by verse, with repeated listening at home. The child moves forward in hifz at their natural pace, which keeps motivation high.

### Learning to read in parallel
Separately, the child works on reading skills at their own pace: letters, vowels, then words. Over time the two paths meet: the child begins reading verses they already know by heart, which is much easier because the sound is familiar and supports the decoding.

This approach means a dyslexic child does not have to wait until their reading is "good enough" before enjoying the Quran.

## Principle 2: Multisensory Learning

Multisensory teaching, using sight, sound, touch and movement together, is one of the most widely recommended approaches for dyslexia. For Arabic letters:

### Letters in sand and clay
The child traces the letter in a tray of sand or salt while saying its sound, or shapes it from play-dough. Feeling the shape strengthens memory.

### Large textured letters
Letters cut from sandpaper or felt, traced with a finger while saying the sound.

### Movement with sound
A gesture for each vowel: hand up for fatha, hand down for kasra, a pushed-forward fist for damma. The body remembers what the eye forgets.

### Colour for dots
Colour the dots of similar letters: two dots above in blue for ta', one dot below in red for ba'. Then count the dots with a finger before pronouncing. Gradually remove the colour.

## Principle 3: The Right Mushaf and Page

### Larger print and more space
Use a large-print Mushaf, or enlarged photocopies of the page being studied. More space between lines helps a lot.

### A reading window
Cut a strip of card with a rectangular window the size of one line or one verse, and slide it down the page. Everything else is hidden, so the page stops being overwhelming and the child does not lose the line.

### Colour-coded Tajweed Mushaf
Some children find colour-coded Tajweed Mushafs helpful because the colours break up the page; others find them distracting. Try both.

### Background colour
Some children with dyslexia find reading easier on cream or pastel-coloured paper rather than bright white. If your child's school uses coloured overlays, try the same colour with the Mushaf pages.

## Principle 4: Gradual Steps in Reading the Mushaf

1. Letter cards → syllable cards → word cards from a verse they know.
2. Reading a verse they have memorized, using the reading window.
3. Reading a full short surah they know.
4. Reading a new short verse slowly, with the teacher.
5. Reading a new half page, a line at a time.

At each step, aim for accuracy first. Speed comes much later, and pushing for speed too early usually increases guessing.

## Principle 5: Short Sessions and Frequent Success

Reading is tiring for a dyslexic child. Short sessions of 10 to 15 minutes, ending with something they can do well (such as reciting a surah they love from memory), keep them feeling capable.

## Common Reading Errors and How to Help

| Error | Example | How to help |
|---|---|---|
| Confusing letters with similar dots | Reads «بيت» as «تيت» | Colour the dots and count them with a finger before saying the sound |
| Confusing similar sounds | Reads sad as sin, ta' as ta | Pair drills: «سَ صَ», «تَ طَ», with a mirror and a teacher |
| Reversing letter order | Reads «ربّ» as «برّ» | Track the word right to left with a finger, letter by letter |
| Dropping a middle letter | «الستقيم» instead of «المستقيم» | Split into syllables: «المُسْ - تَ - قيم» |
| Guessing from the first letter | Says a word they know instead of the printed one | Cover the word and reveal it one letter at a time |
| Losing the line | Skips or repeats a line | Reading window or a ruler under the line |

## A Sample Weekly Plan

| Day | Memorization (by ear) | Reading (gradual) | Multisensory activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saturday | New verse with talqeen | Word cards from the verse | Trace a tricky letter in sand |
| Sunday | Revision and listening | Read the memorized verse in print | Play-dough letters |
| Monday | Lesson with the teacher | Reading with the window | Colour game for similar letters |
| Tuesday | New verse | Word cards | Textured letters |
| Wednesday | Revision | Read one line with help | "Spot the letter" game |
| Thursday | Lesson with the teacher | Lesson with the teacher | Recite to a parent |
| Friday | Listen to the surah | Rest | The surah's story |

## The Teacher's Role

A good Quran teacher for a child with dyslexia:
- understands that slow reading is not laziness;
- corrects gently, focusing on one or two error types at a time;
- uses multisensory tools and visual supports;
- keeps lessons short and varied;
- celebrates memorization strengths so the child never feels "bad at Quran";
- communicates with parents about which letters and patterns to practise at home.

One-to-one online lessons can work very well, because the teacher can share an enlarged, highlighted page on screen and focus entirely on one child's reading.

## Technology That Helps

- **Mushaf apps with audio and word-by-word highlighting**: the child hears each word as it is highlighted, linking sound and shape.
- **Zoom and line-focus features** on tablets to enlarge one line at a time.
- **Recording**: the child records their reading and compares it with the teacher's recording.

Apps support learning; they do not replace a teacher who listens and corrects.

## When Will You See Progress?

Progress in reading is usually slower than progress in memorization, and that is fine. Typical early signs:
- Fewer confusions between dotted letters.
- Reading a familiar verse without the reading window.
- Self-correcting before the teacher points out an error.
- Less anxiety when the Mushaf is opened.

Measure against the child's own starting point, never against siblings or classmates.

## Protecting Your Child's Confidence

Children with dyslexia often hear a lot of correction at school. The Quran lesson should be a place where they feel successful and loved:
- Praise effort specifically: "You checked the dots before reading. That's exactly right."
- Never compare reading speed with siblings.
- Let them recite from memory in front of family, where they shine.
- Remind them of the hadith: "The one who recites the Quran and stumbles over it, finding it difficult, will have two rewards" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). Their struggle is not a failure; it is a doubled reward.

Allah has made the Quran easy to remember:

﴿وَلَقَدۡ يَسَّرۡنَا ٱلۡقُرۡءَانَ لِلذِّكۡرِ فَهَلۡ مِن مُّدَّكِرٖ﴾ (Quran 54:17)

Meaning: "And We have certainly made the Quran easy for remembrance, so is there any who will remember?" Many children who find reading hard discover that memorizing the Quran is one of the things they do best.

## Working With School and Specialists

- Share the school's assessment and recommendations with the Quran teacher.
- Ask the specialist which strategies they use in English reading; many transfer to Arabic (multisensory work, overlays, chunking).
- Tell the school about your child's Quran memorization achievements. It can boost how teachers see them.

## A Note for Teachers: Adapting the Lesson Structure

If you teach a child with dyslexia, a few small changes to your usual lesson make a big difference:
- **Open with success**: start every lesson with something the child does well, such as reciting a memorized surah, before any reading.
- **One error type per lesson**: decide in advance which pattern you will focus on (for example, dotted letters), and let minor errors in other areas pass for now.
- **Explicit, not assumed**: don't assume the child will "pick up" patterns by exposure. Explain them clearly, show them, and practise them in short bursts.
- **Over-learning**: revisit the same letters and words many times across lessons. Children with dyslexia often need more repetition before a skill becomes automatic.
- **End with a summary**: one sentence of what was learned today and one thing to practise at home.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a child with dyslexia memorize the whole Quran?
Yes, many have. Memorization relies heavily on listening and repetition, which can be strengths for dyslexic children. Reading may develop more slowly, and that is fine.

### Should we stop reading practice and only memorize?
No. Keep both paths: memorization by ear for progress and motivation, and gentle, structured reading practice in parallel.

### Does the Uthmani script make it harder?
It adds some visual information, such as small letters and signs, but with a large-print Mushaf, a reading window and gradual teaching, most children adapt.

### Are online lessons suitable for a dyslexic child?
Often very suitable: one-to-one attention, an enlarged page on screen, and a calm home environment.

### How do I know if it's dyslexia or just a normal delay?
Only a specialist assessment can tell. Persistent difficulty across both English and Arabic reading, despite good teaching, is a reason to ask for one.

### How many lessons a week suit a dyslexic child?
Two or three short lessons a week, plus 10 minutes of daily practice at home, is a good starting point.

### Does writing the letters by hand help?
Yes. Writing while saying the sound is a strong multisensory activity. Keep it short and fun rather than a long copying exercise.

### Should my child use a transliteration instead of the Arabic script?
Transliteration can be a temporary support for memorizing by ear, but it is not a substitute for learning to read the Arabic text, and it can create pronunciation errors. Use it sparingly, and keep building Arabic reading skills alongside.
