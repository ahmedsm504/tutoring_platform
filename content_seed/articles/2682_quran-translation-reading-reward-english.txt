Millions of Muslims around the world do not speak Arabic. Many of them read the Quran in English, Urdu, Turkish, Indonesian or another language, and they ask sincere questions: When I read the English translation, do I get the reward for every letter? Can I pray in English until I learn Arabic? Is it acceptable to read the Quran in transliteration? And how can I recite in Arabic and still understand what I am reciting?

This article answers these questions clearly, and offers a practical plan to combine the blessing of recitation with the light of understanding.

## The Quran and a Translation: What Is the Difference?
**The Quran** is the speech of Allah revealed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ **in Arabic words**. It is recited as an act of worship, inimitable in its wording and meaning, and transmitted by mass transmission across generations. Allah says:

﴿إِنَّآ أَنزَلۡنَٰهُ قُرۡءَٰنًا عَرَبِيّٗا لَّعَلَّكُمۡ تَعۡقِلُونَ﴾ (Quran 12:2)

**A translation** conveys the **meanings** of the Quran in another language according to the translator's understanding. That is why scholars call it a "translation of the meanings of the Quran" rather than "the Quran in English", because the inimitable wording of the Quran cannot be carried into another language. What is carried is what the translator understood of its meaning.

This difference leads to important rulings:
1. **A translation is not the Quran**, so it does not take all the rulings of the Quran.
2. **A translation is human work**, which can be right or wrong, and translators differ.
3. **The inimitability lies in the Arabic wording**, which cannot be transferred. However excellent a translation is, it cannot carry the eloquence of the original.

## Does Reading a Translation Bring the Reward of Recitation?
The special reward for recitation in the hadith of Ibn Mas'ud, "Whoever recites a letter from the Book of Allah will have a good deed for it, and a good deed is multiplied by ten," reported by at-Tirmidhi who graded it authentic, is **connected to reciting the Quran itself** in its Arabic wording. The hadith gives the example of the letters Alif, Lam, Mim. A translation is not the letters of the Quran, so this specific letter-by-letter reward is not achieved by reading it.

But **reading a translation is another great act of worship** with its own reward, because it is:
- **Seeking knowledge** of the meanings of Allah's words, and seeking knowledge is one of the best ways to draw near to Allah.
- **Reflection** on what Allah revealed, and reflection is the great purpose of the Quran.
- **A means to action**, and acting on the Quran is the purpose for which it was revealed.

﴿كِتَٰبٌ أَنزَلۡنَٰهُ إِلَيۡكَ مُبَٰرَكࣱ لِّيَدَّبَّرُوٓاْ ءَايَٰتِهِۦ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُوْلُواْ ٱلۡأَلۡبَٰبِ﴾ (Quran 38:29)

So a Muslim who reads a translation to understand is rewarded, by Allah's permission, with the reward of the seeker of knowledge and the one who reflects, even if not with the letter-by-letter reward of recitation. The most complete path is to combine both: recite the Arabic to gain the reward of its letters, and read the translation to gain the reward of understanding.

## Can You Pray Using a Translation?
### The majority view
The majority of scholars, the Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali schools, hold that **reciting the Quran in prayer in a language other than Arabic is not valid**, and that a translation of Al-Fatihah does not count. Al-Fatihah is a pillar of the prayer, Al-Fatihah is Quran, and the Quran is its Arabic wording; a translation is not Quran.

### Abu Hanifah's view and his reported change
It is reported that **Imam Abu Hanifah** at one time permitted reciting in Persian in prayer for someone who could not recite Arabic, and many Hanafi scholars report that he **returned** to the view of his two students, that it is not permitted for someone able to recite Arabic. The Hanafi school settled on the position that someone able to recite in Arabic must not recite in another language.

### What does a new Muslim do before learning Al-Fatihah?
This is a very practical question, and the answer is in the Sunnah. Abu Dawud and an-Nasa'i reported from 'Abdullah ibn Abi Awfa that a man came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "I cannot learn anything of the Quran, so teach me something that will suffice me." He said: "Say: *Subhan Allah, wal-hamdu lillah, wa la ilaha illallah, wallahu akbar, wa la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah*" (Glory be to Allah, praise be to Allah, there is no god but Allah, Allah is the Greatest, and there is no power and no strength except through Allah). The hadith is graded hasan.

So a new Muslim should:
1. **Start learning Al-Fatihah in Arabic immediately**. It is seven short verses that can be memorised in days.
2. **Until then, say this remembrance** in place of the recitation, and the prayer is valid.
3. **Recite whatever part of Al-Fatihah they have learned**, and complete it with this remembrance.
4. **Never delay the prayer** on the excuse that they have not learned yet.

## Reading the Quran in Transliteration
Some books and websites write the Quran in Latin letters, so that a non-Arab reads "Bismillahir-Rahmanir-Rahim" without knowing the Arabic letters. What is the ruling?

Many scholars and fiqh councils hold that **it is not permitted to write the Quran in non-Arabic letters as a Mushaf**, for several reasons:
1. **Latin letters cannot capture Arabic sounds**: Ḥa and Ha, Ayn and Hamzah, Ṣad and Sin, Ḍad and Dal are often written with the same Latin letter, leading to clear mistakes that change the meaning.
2. **The Uthmani script is fixed by transmission**, according to the majority, and may not be changed.
3. **Fear of distortion**, given the many different transliteration systems.

Some scholars permit transliteration **temporarily for teaching**, alongside the Arabic text, to help a beginner, while stressing that it is a bridge and does not replace learning the Arabic letters.

**Practical advice**: the twenty-eight Arabic letters can be learned in a few weeks with a foundational course such as the Noorani Qaida, and then a Muslim can read directly from the Mushaf. This is more correct, and easier in the long run.

## How Can a Non-Arab Combine Recitation and Understanding?
### Stage 1: The letters and Al-Fatihah
Learn the Arabic letters and their sounds with a teacher, and memorise Al-Fatihah correctly. Read its meaning in your language, so every prayer becomes a conversation you understand.

### Stage 2: Daily double reading
Each day, recite a page in Arabic, then read its translation, then recite it again in Arabic with the meaning in mind. This simple routine gives you both rewards and builds understanding steadily.

### Stage 3: Word meanings
A relatively small number of words make up a large share of the Quran's text. Learn the most frequent words gradually, a few each day, using word-by-word resources. Soon you will recognise words as you recite.

### Stage 4: Tafsir
Read a reliable, simple tafsir in your language, surah by surah, starting with the surahs you recite most in prayer.

## How to Choose a Reliable Translation
1. **It should come from a known institution** or a translator trusted in creed and knowledge.
2. **It should be reviewed by scholars** who know both Arabic and the target language.
3. **It should preserve the meaning** without inserting the translator's personal opinions into the main text.
4. **Explanatory notes should be separate** from the translation itself.
5. **It should be printed alongside the Arabic text**, so the reader can return to the original.
6. **Beware of translations produced by deviant sects** that twist meanings to fit their beliefs.

## Why the Quran Cannot Be Translated Word for Word
Arabic words often carry layers of meaning that one English word cannot hold. For example, *ar-Rahman* and *ar-Rahim* both come from the root of mercy, but differ in emphasis and scope, and translators struggle to capture the difference in two English words. The Quran's rhythm, its sound patterns, its concise structure and its rhetorical devices are also lost in translation. That is why even the best translation is described as a translation of meanings, not the Quran itself.

## Other Rulings Related to Translations
- **Touching a translation without wudu**: if the translation is printed alone without the Arabic text, many scholars permit touching it without wudu, because it is not a Mushaf. If it includes the Arabic text, it is more cautious to have wudu.
- **Giving a translation to a non-Muslim**: permitted and encouraged as a means of inviting them to Islam, unlike the Arabic Mushaf, which the majority hold should not be given to a non-Muslim to handle.
- **Quoting translations**: permitted in talks and books for non-Arabs, noting that it is a translation of the meaning.
- **Respect for translations**: a translation carries the meanings of Allah's words, so it should be treated with respect and not discarded carelessly.

## A Message to Non-Arab Muslims
Do not feel that the Quran belongs only to Arabs. Most Muslims in the world today are not Arabs, and throughout history some of the greatest scholars of the Quran, its recitations and its language were not Arabs by birth. Allah made His Book easy to remember:

﴿وَلَقَدۡ يَسَّرۡنَا ٱلۡقُرۡءَانَ لِلذِّكۡرِ فَهَلۡ مِن مُّدَّكِرٖ﴾ (Quran 54:17)

Your effort to learn its letters, your stumbling over its words, and your tears when you finally understand a verse are all precious. The Prophet ﷺ said, as reported in al-Bukhari and Muslim, that the one who recites the Quran and stumbles over it, finding it difficult, has two rewards.

## For Parents in Non-Arabic-Speaking Countries
- Teach your children the Arabic letters early, alongside their school language.
- Read the translation of short surahs with them so they understand what they memorise.
- Find a teacher who can teach both correct recitation and meaning.
- Make Arabic a living part of home life: simple words, du'as and phrases.

## Common Mistakes
- **Reading only transliteration for years** without moving on to the Arabic letters.
- **Ignoring the translation completely**: the opposite mistake, where a Muslim recites the Quran all their life without understanding a single verse.
- **Treating a translation as the Quran itself**, quoting it as if it were the exact words of Allah.
- **Choosing a translation at random** without checking its source.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### Is there reward for reading the Quran in English?
Yes, the reward of seeking knowledge and reflection; the special letter-by-letter reward of recitation is connected to reciting the Arabic.

### Can I pray in English?
Not according to the majority of scholars. A new Muslim who cannot yet recite Al-Fatihah says the remembrance taught in the hadith until they learn it.

### What does a new Muslim say in prayer before learning Al-Fatihah?
"Subhan Allah, wal-hamdu lillah, wa la ilaha illallah, wallahu akbar, wa la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah," as in the hadith of 'Abdullah ibn Abi Awfa.

### Is reading the Quran in transliteration allowed?
It is not considered a Mushaf; some scholars permit it temporarily for learning alongside the Arabic, but learning the Arabic letters quickly is best.

### Can I touch an English translation without wudu?
Yes, if it does not contain the Arabic text, according to many scholars.

### Should I read the translation before or after reciting?
Recite the Arabic first, then read the translation, then recite the Arabic again with the meaning in mind.

### How long does it take to learn to read Arabic letters?
Usually from a few weeks to a few months, depending on regularity, with a teacher and a foundational course.

## Conclusion
A translation is not the Quran but a rendering of its meanings, so reading it does not bring the special reward of reciting its letters, and the majority of scholars do not accept it in prayer. But reading it to understand is a great act of worship: seeking knowledge and reflecting on Allah's words. The most complete path for a non-Arab is to learn the Arabic letters quickly, recite the Quran in its own words, and read a reliable translation alongside it, so that both the tongue and the heart receive their share of the Book of Allah.
