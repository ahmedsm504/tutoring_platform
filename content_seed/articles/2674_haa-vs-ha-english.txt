Say "Alhamdulillah" the way most English speakers do, and you will probably use an ordinary English "h". But the "h" in *al-ḥamdu* is not the English "h" at all. It is the Arabic letter **Ḥa (ح)**, a squeezed, breathy sound from the middle of the throat. Arabic also has another "h", **Ha (ه)**, which is much closer to English. Because English has only one "h", learners tend to pronounce both Arabic letters the same way, and that changes words.

This guide explains the two letters, how to produce each one, how to tell them apart, and how to practise with the words you recite every day.

## Why It Matters
Mixing Ḥa and Ha changes the word:

| With Ḥa (ح) | Meaning | With Ha (ه) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| حَمْد (ḥamd) | praise | هَمْد (hamd) | stillness, lifelessness |
| حَلَك (ḥalak) | intense darkness | هَلَك (halak) | perished |
| سَحَر (saḥar) | pre-dawn | سَهَر (sahar) | staying awake |

The first example is enough to show the problem: *al-ḥamdu lillah* means "all praise is due to Allah", while pronouncing it with an English-style "h" moves it towards a different root. In recitation, changing a letter is a clear mistake (*lahn jali*), so learners should work to master the distinction, while remembering the hadith in al-Bukhari and Muslim that the one who struggles with the Quran has two rewards.

## Where Each Letter Comes From
The throat has three regions, each producing two letters:

| Region | Letters |
|---|---|
| Deepest part (near the chest) | Hamzah (ء) and **Ha (ه)** |
| Middle | Ayn (ع) and **Ḥa (ح)** |
| Top (near the mouth) | Ghayn (غ) and Kha (خ) |

### Ha (ه): the deepest point, soft and open
Ha comes from the **deepest part of the throat**. It is a soft, open breath with the throat relaxed, very similar to the English "h" in "house" or "hat". If you can say "hello", you can say Ha.

### Ḥa (ح): the middle of the throat, squeezed
Ḥa comes from the **middle of the throat**, the same region as Ayn. The muscles there tighten, and the breath is forced through a narrow passage, creating a strong, raspy, whispered sound. It is like the sound you make when you breathe hard onto glasses to clean them, but with the throat squeezed more tightly, or the sound of a person exhaling with relief after drinking something very hot.

## Characteristics of Each Letter
| Characteristic | Ha (ه) | Ḥa (ح) |
|---|---|---|
| Place | Deepest throat | Middle throat |
| Throat muscles | Relaxed | Tightened |
| Voiced or whispered | Whispered | Whispered |
| Strength of breath | Soft | Strong and raspy |
| Heavy or light | Light | Light |

Both letters are **whispered** (*hams*) and **light**. The difference is mainly the **place** and the **tightness** of the throat.

## Exercises
### Exercise 1: The two breaths
1. Breathe out gently through an open mouth: "haaa". This is Ha.
2. Now tighten the middle of your throat, as if beginning to whisper loudly, and breathe out with force: "ḥaaa". This is Ḥa.
3. Alternate: ha, ḥa, ha, ḥa.

### Exercise 2: From Ayn to Ḥa
If you have learned Ayn (ع), you already know the place of Ḥa. Say Ayn, then remove your voice and keep the breath: the result is Ḥa. Ayn and Ḥa are twins: same place, but Ayn is voiced and Ḥa is breathy.

### Exercise 3: The mirror and the hand
Hold your hand in front of your mouth. With Ha you feel a soft, warm breath. With Ḥa you feel a stronger, more forced stream of air, and you can hear a rasp.

### Exercise 4: Ḥa with sukun
Ḥa with sukun is where many learners lose it. Practise:
- *Al-ḥamdu* (الْحَمْدُ): Ḥa with sukun, after the lam.
- *Yuḥyi* (يُحْيِي): Ḥa with sukun before Ya.
- *Fa-sabbiḥ* (فَسَبِّحْ) when stopping: Ḥa with sukun at the end.

Hold the raspy breath for a moment; do not let it disappear.

### Exercise 5: Ha at the end of words
Ha at the end of a word, especially when stopping, is often dropped by English speakers because English "h" never comes at the end:
- *Allah* (اللّٰه) when stopping: the final Ha must be heard as a soft breath.
- *'alayh* (عليه) when stopping.
- *Rabbih* (ربه) when stopping, as in Al-'Adiyat and Al-Bayyinah.

## Practice Words from Al-Fatihah
﴿بِسۡمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحۡمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ﴾ ﴿ٱلۡحَمۡدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلۡعَٰلَمِينَ﴾ ﴿ٱلرَّحۡمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ﴾ (Quran 1:1-3)

- **Bismillah ar-Raḥman ar-Raḥim**: both *Raḥman* and *Raḥim* have **Ḥa**, squeezed and strong. Many learners say "Rahman" with an English "h". Correcting this alone improves every recitation of the Basmalah.
- **Al-ḥamdu lillah**: Ḥa with sukun.
- **Lillah**: ends with a soft Ha.

﴿ٱهۡدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلۡمُسۡتَقِيمَ﴾ ﴿صِرَٰطَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنۡعَمۡتَ عَلَيۡهِمۡ غَيۡرِ ٱلۡمَغۡضُوبِ عَلَيۡهِمۡ وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ﴾ (Quran 1:6-7)

- **Ihdina**: Ha (ه), soft. Notice the contrast with *Al-ḥamdu*.
- **'alayhim**: Ha (ه), soft.

## Practice Words from Short Surahs
| Word | Letter | Surah |
|---|---|---|
| أَحَد (aḥad) | Ḥa | Al-Ikhlas |
| هُوَ (huwa) | Ha | Al-Ikhlas |
| فَسَبِّحْ (fasabbiḥ) | Ḥa | An-Nasr |
| رَبِّهِم (rabbihim) | Ha | Al-'Adiyat |
| الْحَاسِد (al-ḥasid) | Ḥa | Al-Falaq |
| حَمَّالَة (ḥammalah) | Ḥa | Al-Masad |
| لَهَب (lahab) | Ha | Al-Masad |

Surah Al-Masad is excellent for practice because it contains both: *Abi Lahab* (Ha), *ḥammalat al-ḥaṭab* (two Ḥas), and *fi jidiha ḥablun min masad* (Ḥa).

## Common Mistakes
### 1. Ḥa pronounced as English "h"
The most common mistake: *Raḥman* becomes "Rahman", *aḥad* becomes "ahad". The Ḥa loses its squeeze.

### 2. Ḥa pronounced as Kha (خ)
Some learners, trying to make Ḥa strong, push it too high and it becomes a scraping Kha: *aḥad* becomes "akhad". Ḥa is in the middle of the throat, smooth and raspy, not scraped.

### 3. Ha dropped at the end of words
"Alla" instead of "Allah" when stopping. The final Ha must be heard.

### 4. Ha made too strong
After learning Ḥa, some learners make every Ha squeezed. Ha must stay soft and relaxed.

### 5. Confusing Ḥa with Ayn
Adding voice to Ḥa so that *aḥad* sounds like *a'ad*. Ḥa is whispered; keep the vocal cords still.

## A Ten-Day Plan
- **Days 1-2**: Exercise 1 (the two breaths), five minutes a day.
- **Days 3-4**: Ḥa with vowels: *ḥa, ḥu, ḥi*, and words like *Raḥman*, *aḥad*.
- **Days 5-6**: Ḥa with sukun: *al-ḥamdu*, *yuḥyi*, *fasabbiḥ*.
- **Days 7-8**: Ha at the end of words: *Allah*, *'alayh*, *rabbih*.
- **Days 9-10**: Recite Al-Fatihah, Al-Ikhlas and Al-Masad slowly, focusing only on the two letters, then recite them to a teacher.

## Learn by Listening
Listen to a skilled reciter recite the Basmalah slowly, and pay attention only to *Raḥman* and *Raḥim*. Then listen to *Ihdina* and *'alayhim*. Your ear will begin to separate the two sounds, and your tongue will follow. A teacher can then confirm whether your Ḥa is in the right place, which is very hard to judge alone.

## The Ha of the Pronoun: A Special Case
In the Quran, the letter Ha (ه) very often appears as a **pronoun** meaning "him" or "his", as in *bihi* (بِهِ, with it), *lahu* (لَهُ, for him) and *rabbahu* (رَبَّهُ, his Lord). This pronoun Ha has a special rule in tajweed called **silah** (connection): when it comes between two vowelled letters, its vowel is lengthened slightly, so *lahu* becomes *lahū* and *bihi* becomes *bihī*. You will see a small waw or ya after the Ha in the Mushaf indicating this.

﴿وَلَمۡ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدُۢ﴾ (Quran 112:4)

In *wa lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad*, the Ha in *lahu* is lengthened by two counts because it sits between the Lam with fatha and the Kaf with damma. Notice also that this verse contains both letters: Ha in *lahu* and Ḥa in *aḥad*. It is a perfect sentence to practise the contrast.

Getting the pronoun Ha right matters because it appears thousands of times in the Quran. Keep it soft and relaxed, and lengthen it only where the silah rule applies.

## When Ha and Ḥa Meet Other Throat Letters
Some sequences place two throat letters close together, which demands careful control:
- **Ayn then Ha**: as in *ma'ahum* (مَعَهُمْ). The voiced squeeze of Ayn is followed by a soft, open breath.
- **Ḥa with sukun before another letter**: as in *wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbik* (وسبح بحمد ربك): keep the Ḥa of *sabbiḥ* audible before moving to the Ba, and squeeze again for the Ḥa of *ḥamdi*.
- **Two Ḥas close together**: as in *ḥammalat al-ḥaṭab* in Surah Al-Masad. Keep both squeezed, without letting the second weaken.
- **Ha after a long vowel**: as in *ilāhu* or *yā ayyuhā*: keep the Ha soft and do not swallow it into the vowel.

These sequences are exactly where English speakers tend to blur letters together. Slow, deliberate practice of a few verses containing them will strengthen your control.

## The Basmalah: Your Daily Practice
The Basmalah, *Bismillah ar-Raḥman ar-Raḥim*, is recited before almost every surah and countless daily actions. It contains two Ḥas, one Ha, and a Lam of the name of Allah. If you make it your daily pronunciation exercise for a month, saying it slowly and correctly every time you eat, enter your home or begin reading, you will have practised Ḥa hundreds of times in context. Many teachers say that a student's Basmalah reveals their throat letters immediately, so perfecting it is a small habit with a large effect.

## Frequently Asked Questions
### What is the difference between ح and ه?
ه (Ha) comes from the deepest part of the throat and is soft like the English "h"; ح (Ḥa) comes from the middle of the throat and is squeezed and raspy.

### Is the "h" in Alhamdulillah ح or ه?
It is ح (Ḥa), the squeezed letter: *al-ḥamdu*.

### Is the "h" in Rahman ح or ه?
It is ح (Ḥa): *ar-Raḥman* and *ar-Raḥim*.

### What is the difference between Ḥa and Kha?
Ḥa comes from the middle of the throat and is smooth and raspy; Kha comes from the top of the throat and is scraped.

### What is the difference between Ḥa and Ayn?
They come from the same place, but Ayn is voiced and Ḥa is whispered.

### Which surah is best for practising both letters?
Surah Al-Masad, which contains Ha in *Lahab* and several Ḥas in *ḥammalat al-ḥaṭab* and *ḥablun*.

### Why do I see a small waw after the Ha in lahu?
It marks *silah*: the vowel of the pronoun Ha is lengthened by two counts when it sits between two vowelled letters.

### Is the h in Allah ح or ه?
It is ه (Ha), the soft letter, and it should be clearly heard when you stop on the name.

### Can I learn these letters without a teacher?
You can make good progress alone with the exercises and by listening to a skilled reciter, but a teacher can confirm your pronunciation much faster.

### Why do Arabs from some regions pronounce Ḥa differently?
Everyday dialects vary, but in Quran recitation both letters are pronounced from their proper places as transmitted by the reciters.

### How long does it take to learn Ḥa?
Most learners can produce a clear Ḥa within a week or two of daily practice, especially if they have already learned Ayn.

## Conclusion
Ha (ه) is the soft "h" you already know; Ḥa (ح) is a new sound, squeezed and raspy, from the middle of the throat. The difference runs through the most recited words in Islam: *ar-Raḥman, ar-Raḥim, al-ḥamdu*. Train the two breaths, practise Ḥa with sukun and Ha at the end of words, use Al-Fatihah and Al-Masad as your daily practice, and let a teacher confirm your progress. When *al-ḥamdu lillah* finally carries its true Ḥa, your praise will sound the way it was revealed.
