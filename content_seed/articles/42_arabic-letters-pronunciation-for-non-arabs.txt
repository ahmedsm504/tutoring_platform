## The 10 Arabic Letters Non-Arabs Find Hardest in Quran Recitation (and How to Fix Them)

If English (or French, German, Urdu...) is your first language, some Arabic sounds will feel completely new. That's normal. The good news: every one of them can be learned with the right explanation and a bit of daily practice. Here are the ten letters our non-Arabic-speaking students struggle with most.

### A simple test for any letter
Scholars of Tajweed give a neat trick: put a vowelled *hamza* (a) before the letter and make the letter silent, like **"ab"**, **"aq"**, **"aḥ"**. Where the sound stops in your mouth or throat is that letter's point of articulation (*makhraj*).

### 1. ح — Ḥaa (from the middle of the throat)
- **Typical mistake:** pronouncing it like an English "h" (ه), so ﴿ٱلۡحَمۡدُ﴾ sounds like *al-hamd*.
- **Fix:** tighten the middle of your throat, as if you're breathing on glasses to clean them, but stronger and with friction. It's a breathy, "squeezed" h.

### 2. ع — ʿAyn (from the middle of the throat)
- **Typical mistake:** dropping it or turning it into a glottal stop (ء), so ﴿نَعۡبُدُ﴾ sounds like *na'bud*.
- **Fix:** start from the Ḥaa position, then add voice. Many learners find it helps to say "aaa" while gently tightening the throat, as if you were about to gag slightly.

### 3. ق — Qaaf (from the very back of the tongue)
- **Typical mistake:** saying it like an English "k".
- **Fix:** raise the very back of your tongue to touch the soft palate, further back than for "k". It produces a deeper, "heavier" sound. Try ﴿قُلۡ﴾ (*qul*) versus "cool".

### 4. خ — Khaa and 5. غ — Ghayn (from the top of the throat)
- **Khaa** is like the "ch" in Scottish *loch* or German *Bach*: voiceless and raspy.
- **Ghayn** is its voiced partner, similar to a French gargled "r".
- **Typical mistake:** turning خ into "k" and غ into "g".

### 6. ض — Ḍaad (from the side of the tongue)
The letter Arabic is famous for, often called *lughat aḍ-ḍād*, "the language of Ḍaad".
- **Typical mistake:** saying it like a heavy "d" or like ظ.
- **Fix:** press **the side of your tongue** (usually the left) against your upper back teeth, and let the sound stretch along the tongue's edge. It is heavy (emphatic) and longer than a "d". Practise with ﴿وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ﴾.

### 7. ص — Ṣaad, 8. ط — Ṭaa, 9. ظ — Ẓaa (the emphatic letters)
These are "heavy" versions of س, ت and ذ.
- **Typical mistake:** pronouncing them light, so ﴿ٱلصِّرَٰطَ﴾ sounds like *as-sirāt* with a thin "s" and "t".
- **Fix:** raise the back of your tongue towards the roof of your mouth while pronouncing them. Your mouth should feel "full". Compare *sīn* and *ṣād* back to back.

### 10. ث — Thaa and ذ — Dhaal
- **Thaa** is the "th" in *think*; **Dhaal** is the "th" in *this*.
- **Typical mistake:** speakers of some languages (and many Arabic dialects!) turn them into "s/t" and "z/d".
- **Fix:** let the tip of your tongue touch the edges of your upper front teeth, slightly sticking out.

### Quick reference table
| Letter | Closest sound | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| ح | breathy, squeezed "h" | middle of throat |
| ع | voiced version of ح | middle of throat |
| ق | deep "k" | back of tongue + soft palate |
| خ | "ch" in *loch* | top of throat |
| غ | gargled French "r" | top of throat |
| ض | heavy, stretched "d" | side of tongue + molars |
| ص ط ظ | heavy s / t / dh | tongue tip, with raised back of tongue |
| ث ذ | "th" in *think* / *this* | tongue tip + upper teeth edges |

### A 10-minute daily routine
1. Pick **two letters** per week.
2. Say each one with the "a + silent letter" test, ten times.
3. Read three Quranic words containing each letter.
4. Record yourself and compare with a reciter such as Sheikh Al-Husary on our [Quran pages](/quran/).

## A Bedtime Quran Routine for Kids: 10 Minutes Before Sleep

Bedtime is one of the best moments to connect children with the Quran: they are calm, close to you, and ready to listen.

### The 10-minute routine
| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | The three Quls with blowing into the hands and wiping |
| 2–4 | Ayat al-Kursi |
| 4–8 | A short Quran story |
| 8–10 | The sleep du'a and a hug |

### Step 1: The three Quls
- The Prophet ﷺ would cup his hands, blow lightly into them, recite **Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas**, and wipe over as much of his body as he could, three times [Al-Bukhari].
- Do it **with** your child at first.

### Step 2: Ayat al-Kursi
- Whoever recites it before sleep has a guardian from Allah until morning [Al-Bukhari].
- Read [Ayat al-Kursi Meaning](/blog/remember-allah-as-you-remember-fathers-english/#Ayat-al-Kursi-Meaning-The-Greatest-Ayah-in-the-Quran-Explain).

### Step 3: A short story
- One prophet story each night: Yunus and the whale, Ibrahim and the fire, Musa and the sea.

### Step 4: The sleep du'a
- «Bismika Allāhumma amūtu wa aḥyā» — *In Your name, O Allah, I die and I live* [Al-Bukhari].

### Tips
- **Same time, same place** every night.
- **No screens** for the last half hour.
- **Let them lead** one step when they're ready.

## 10 Mistakes Beginner Quran Memorizers Make (and How to Fix Them)

Starting hifz is exciting, but small mistakes early on can cost months later. Here are ten to avoid.

| # | Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Memorizing before correcting pronunciation | Recite the page to a teacher first |
| 2 | Taking too much at once | Start with 5 lines a day |
| 3 | Skipping revision | Revise more than you memorize |
| 4 | Switching Mushaf editions | Use one Madinah Mushaf edition |
| 5 | Only listening, never reading | Combine reading, listening and reciting |
| 6 | Ignoring similar verses | Note them in a notebook |
| 7 | No fixed time | Tie hifz to a prayer, like after Fajr |
| 8 | Reciting only silently | Recite aloud so you hear mistakes |
| 9 | No one to recite to | Find a teacher or a hifz partner |
| 10 | Comparing yourself to others | Measure progress against yourself |

### The most important rule
- The Prophet ﷺ said: «Keep refreshing your knowledge of the Quran, for it escapes faster than camels from their ropes» [Al-Bukhari and Muslim].
- **New memorization without revision** is the main reason people forget.

### A simple daily structure
| Part | Time |
|---|---|
| Revise old pages | 15 minutes |
| Revise yesterday's page | 5 minutes |
| Memorize the new portion | 15 minutes |

## How to Choose an Online Quran Tutor for Your Child: 10 Questions to Ask First

Online Quran classes are a blessing, especially for families living in non-Arabic-speaking countries. But with so many academies and freelance tutors, choosing can be hard. These questions will help you decide, whether you choose us or someone else.

### 1. Is the teacher certified or truly proficient?
An Ijazah (a certificate with a chain of transmission) isn't required to teach a beginner, but it's a strong sign of mastery. If there's no Ijazah, ask about the teacher's experience and who they studied with. Read [what an Ijazah is](/blog/sun-and-moon-letters-english/#What-Is-an-Ijazah-in-Quran-Types-Requirements-and-How-to-Get).

### 2. Does the teacher have experience with children specifically?
Teaching a five-year-old is completely different from teaching an adult. Good children's teachers know how to switch activities when a child gets bored, use stories and games, and when to stop.

### 3. Is there a free trial class?
The trial is the most important step. Watch for:
- Did the teacher start by assessing your child's level?
- Were they patient and kind?
- Did your child enjoy it and ask for another class?

### 4. One-to-one or group?
One-to-one classes give your child the teacher's full attention. Group classes can suit very social children, but each child recites for much less time.

### 5. What happens between classes?
Memorization doesn't stick from the class alone. Is there homework? Can your child record their recitation and send it to the teacher? Does the teacher reply with corrections and encouragement?

### 6. Will you get regular progress reports?
You have the right to know where your child stands and what needs work. A written monthly report is a sign of a serious, organised academy.

### 7. How is your child protected?
This question is rarely asked, and it's one of the most important:
- Does all communication between teacher and child happen **inside the platform**, or through private phone numbers and social media?
- Does the administration supervise conversations?
- Is there a clear policy against private contact?

At Al-Agmi, all teacher–student communication happens inside our platform. Nobody's phone number is shown to the other party, messages containing numbers or social handles are blocked automatically, and the administration oversees everything.

### 8. How flexible is the schedule?
If you live in a different time zone, make sure class times suit you, and that rescheduling or making up a missed class is possible.

### 9. What's the curriculum?
Where does the child start? How much will they memorize weekly? Do they learn reading and Tajweed alongside memorization? Is there an educational side, such as simple stories of the Prophets?

### 10. What do other parents say?
Ask for real experiences, and talk to parents you know if possible. Real feedback beats any advert.

### Quick checklist
- Proficient teacher with experience teaching children
- Free trial class
- One-to-one sessions
- Homework and follow-up between classes
- Written monthly reports
- Safe, supervised communication
- Flexible times in your time zone
- A clear plan

## Which Reciter Should You Listen to for Memorizing the Quran?

Listening is one of the strongest tools for hifz, but the reciter you choose matters.

### What to look for
| Quality | Why |
|---|---|
| Slow, clear pace (murattal) | You hear every letter and rule |
| Accurate tajweed | You copy what you hear |
| Consistent style | Easier to remember |
| Riwayah Hafs (if you read Hafs) | Matches your Mushaf |

### Teaching vs performance
- **Teaching (muʿallim) recordings** repeat each ayah, sometimes with children repeating after the sheikh. Excellent for beginners.
- **Murattal** is steady recitation, ideal for daily memorization.
- **Mujawwad** is slower and melodic, beautiful to listen to but less practical for hifz.

### Reciters many memorizers use
- **Sheikh Mahmoud Khalil al-Husary**: very clear murattal and a teaching recording.
- **Sheikh Muhammad Siddiq al-Minshawi**: murattal and a teaching recording with children.
- Choose any **trusted reciter** whose style helps you; these are examples, not the only options.

### Stick to one
- Switching reciters often confuses your memory of the melody and the stops.

### Don't depend only on audio
- **Read from the Mushaf** while listening, so you memorize the page as well.
- **Recite to a teacher**: listening does not correct your own mistakes.
