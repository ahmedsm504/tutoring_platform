Every Ramadan, millions of Muslims make the same intention: "This year I will finish the whole Quran." Many succeed. Many others start strongly, fall behind in the second week, and end the month feeling guilty. The difference is rarely sincerity. It is usually planning: knowing exactly how much to read each day, when to read it, and what to do when life gets in the way.

This guide gives you the numbers, ready-made schedules for different lifestyles, practical tips, and a gentle reminder of what matters most. If you prefer, you can skip straight to our [Ramadan khatam schedule](/en/quran/khatam-planner/ramadan/), which builds a day-by-day plan for you.

## Why Ramadan and the Quran Belong Together

Allah describes Ramadan by the Quran before He mentions fasting:

﴿شَهۡرُ رَمَضَانَ ٱلَّذِيٓ أُنزِلَ فِيهِ ٱلۡقُرۡءَانُ هُدٗى لِّلنَّاسِ وَبَيِّنَٰتٖ مِّنَ ٱلۡهُدَىٰ وَٱلۡفُرۡقَانِۚ فَمَن شَهِدَ مِنكُمُ ٱلشَّهۡرَ فَلۡيَصُمۡهُۖ وَمَن كَانَ مَرِيضًا أَوۡ عَلَىٰ سَفَرٖ فَعِدَّةࣱ مِّنۡ أَيَّامٍ أُخَرَۗ يُرِيدُ ٱللَّهُ بِكُمُ ٱلۡيُسۡرَ وَلَا يُرِيدُ بِكُمُ ٱلۡعُسۡرَ وَلِتُكۡمِلُواْ ٱلۡعِدَّةَ وَلِتُكَبِّرُواْ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ مَا هَدَىٰكُمۡ وَلَعَلَّكُمۡ تَشۡكُرُونَ﴾ (Quran 2:185)

Ramadan is the month in which the Quran was revealed. The Prophet ﷺ was at his most generous in Ramadan, and Jibril would meet him every night of Ramadan to study the Quran with him (al-Bukhari and Muslim). So reading the Quran in Ramadan is not an extra activity added to fasting; it is the heart of the month.

The reward is immense. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever reads a letter from the Book of Allah will have a good deed, and a good deed is multiplied by ten. I do not say that Alif-Lam-Mim is one letter, but Alif is a letter, Lam is a letter and Mim is a letter" (at-Tirmidhi, graded sahih by al-Albani).

## The Numbers: How Many Pages a Day?

The standard Madani mushaf has **604 pages**, divided into **30 juz** of about 20 pages each. Here is what different goals require:

| Goal | Pages per day | Per prayer (5 prayers) | Approximate time per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| One khatam in 30 days | 20 pages (one juz) | 4 pages | 60–90 minutes |
| One khatam in 29 days | about 21 pages | 4–5 pages | 65–95 minutes |
| Two khatams | 40 pages | 8 pages | 2–3 hours |
| Three khatams | 60 pages | 12 pages | 3–4.5 hours |
| Half a khatam | 10 pages | 2 pages | 30–45 minutes |

Most people read a page in three to five minutes when reading carefully. If you are still learning to read Arabic, it may take longer, and that is fine.

Because the month may be 29 days, it is wise to read a little extra in the first week, so that you are not left with a juz to catch up on the last night.

## The Five-Prayer Method

The simplest and most reliable method is to attach your reading to the five daily prayers. Four pages after each prayer gives you one juz a day, without needing a single long block of time:

- **After Fajr**: 4 pages. Often the calmest time of day; many people read more here.
- **After Dhuhr**: 4 pages, at work or at home.
- **After Asr**: 4 pages.
- **After Maghrib**: 4 pages, once the iftar rush is over.
- **After Isha or Tarawih**: 4 pages.

Why does this work? Because prayers are fixed points in the day that you already keep. Linking a new habit to an existing one is far stronger than relying on willpower or a free hour that never comes.

## Schedules for Different Lives

### If you work full time
Your real time is before and after work. Make Fajr your main reading time, with 6 to 8 pages. Use your lunch break for 4 pages on a phone app. Read 4 pages after Maghrib and the rest after Tarawih. On weekends, read ahead to build a buffer.

### If you are a parent of young children
Your time comes in fragments. Keep a mushaf in the kitchen and read a page while food is cooking. Read from your phone while feeding a baby. Listen to the day's pages while doing housework, then read them afterwards: listening first makes reading faster and easier. Half a khatam with consistency is better than an ambitious plan you abandon.

### If you are a student
Ramadan may fall during exams. Protect a small, fixed daily portion that never stops, even on exam days, and read more on lighter days. The Quran does not compete with your studies; it clears your mind and brings blessing to your time.

### If you are still learning to read Arabic
Do not let fluency stop you. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The one who is skilled in the Quran will be with the noble and righteous scribes, and the one who reads the Quran and stumbles over it, finding it difficult, will have a double reward" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). Read less, read carefully, and read a translation alongside so you understand. Our guide for [memorizing the Quran as a revert](/blog/quran-hifz-for-reverts-english/) has more advice for learners.

## Quality Before Quantity

The early Muslims were known for reading a great deal in Ramadan, and some reports mention scholars finishing the Quran many times during the month. But they also warned against reading without understanding. Allah says:

﴿كِتَٰبٌ أَنزَلۡنَٰهُ إِلَيۡكَ مُبَٰرَكࣱ لِّيَدَّبَّرُوٓاْ ءَايَٰتِهِۦ وَلِيَتَذَكَّرَ أُوْلُواْ ٱلۡأَلۡبَٰبِ﴾ (Quran 38:29)

The purpose of the Quran is reflection and remembrance. So if you must choose between three rushed khatams and one khatam read with attention and understanding, choose the second. A good balance for many people is to read the daily juz at a moderate pace, and pick **one verse each day** to reflect on more deeply.

### A simple daily reflection routine
1. While reading, mark one verse that touched you.
2. After your reading, look up its meaning in a short tafsir.
3. Ask yourself: what does this verse tell me about Allah, about myself, and about what I should do today?
4. Share it with your family at iftar.

## Reading Speed and Tajweed

Speed matters when you have a big goal, but it should never cost you correct recitation. Scholars describe three speeds: slow and deliberate (tahqiq), moderate (tadwir), and faster but still correct (hadr). Most people finishing one khatam in Ramadan do well with the moderate pace. Learn more in our guide to [the three speeds of Quran recitation](/blog/quran-recitation-speeds-english/).

Whatever your speed, keep the basic rules: lengthen the long vowels to their proper count, keep the nasal sound (ghunnah) on doubled noon and meem, and do not stop in the middle of a phrase in a way that changes the meaning. If you are unsure of your recitation, a single session with a teacher before Ramadan can correct mistakes you would otherwise repeat thousands of times.

## When You Fall Behind

Almost everyone falls behind at some point: an illness, a busy week, guests, travel. Here is what to do:

- **Do not try to catch up in one day.** That leads to exhaustion and giving up.
- **Spread the missed pages over the next week.** If you missed one juz, add 3 extra pages per day for a week.
- **Use the weekend.** Weekends and days off are your catch-up days.
- **Lower the goal without guilt.** If you are far behind, switch to half a khatam this Ramadan and complete the rest in Shawwal. The Prophet ﷺ said that the most beloved deeds to Allah are the most consistent, even if small (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

## The Last Ten Nights

The last ten nights are the peak of Ramadan. The Prophet ﷺ used to strive in them more than at any other time, staying up at night and waking his family (al-Bukhari and Muslim). If you are on track, these nights are perfect for slower, deeper reading in night prayer. If you are behind, use them to catch up, but remember that Laylat al-Qadr is also about du'a, prayer and sincerity, not only page counts. 'A'ishah asked the Prophet ﷺ what to say if she knew which night it was, and he taught her: "O Allah, You are Pardoning and You love to pardon, so pardon me" (at-Tirmidhi, who graded it hasan sahih).

## Reading the Quran as a Family

A family khatam is a beautiful way to involve everyone. Divide the 30 juz among family members according to their ability: an adult might take five juz, a teenager two, a younger child one with help. Agree to finish on the 27th night or before Eid, and make a short du'a together when the last juz is completed.

Many people find it easier to read a juz when they know its main themes in advance. Spending five minutes on a short summary before reading turns twenty pages of Arabic into a journey you can follow. ## Preparing Before Ramadan Begins

The biggest reason people fail is the sudden jump from little or no daily reading to a full juz. If you start preparing in Sha'ban, the month before Ramadan, the jump becomes a gentle slope:

- **Four weeks before**: read two pages a day, every day, at a fixed time.
- **Three weeks before**: correct your recitation, starting with Al-Fatiha, which you will recite hundreds of times in Ramadan.
- **Two weeks before**: raise your reading to ten pages a day and review the surahs you will pray with.
- **One week before**: try one "Ramadan day" with a full juz split across the prayers, and adjust your plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many pages should I read a day to finish the Quran in Ramadan?
About 20 pages a day in the standard 604-page mushaf, which is four pages after each of the five prayers. If the month is 29 days, read slightly more in the first week.

### Is it better to finish the Quran several times or once with reflection?
Both are good, and the early Muslims did both. For most people, one khatam read carefully, with some time each day for understanding, brings more benefit than several rushed readings.

### Does listening to the Quran count as finishing it?
Listening is an act of worship with its own reward, and the Prophet ﷺ loved to hear the Quran recited by others. But a khatam usually means reading. Use listening to support your reading, or as your main connection if you cannot read.

### Can I read from my phone?
Yes. Reading from a phone or tablet is a convenient way to keep up during the day, especially at work or on the move.

### What if I cannot read Arabic at all?
Start learning now, even a few letters a day, and meanwhile read a translation and listen to the Arabic. Many people learn to read the Quran within a few months with a patient teacher.
